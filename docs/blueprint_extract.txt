01   The Thesis
Most candidates apply to quant roles with three or four disconnected notebooks: a CNN on
stock images, a "predict the next close" LSTM, a sentiment scrape, and a Sharpe ratio
computed on in-sample data. A hiring quant reads all four in under ten minutes and concludes
the candidate does not know the research cycle.
The candidate who gets hired has one thing the others do not: a single, end-to-end research
pipeline that runs from raw data to a net-of-cost, capacity-constrained, purged-validated
backtest, with a written economic hypothesis, a documented attempt to falsify it, and a
research note that reads like an internal memo. The portfolio is not a list of projects. It is a
body of work with a spine.
The single most important question the portfolio must answer in every project is not "is this
model accurate?" but "is this edge real, and would it survive being traded?"

(Weeks 3-10)

text, and this predictability is stronger in stocks with high information asymmetry." A
hypothesis like "machine learning predicts stock prices" is not a hypothesis; it is a topic.

Method
1. Build a panel of monthly (or 5-day) rebalance dates with a point-in-time universe.
2. Labels: forward excess return over 1-3 months, plus triple-barrier labels for the event-driven
subset.
3. Features: technical (momentum at multiple horizons, reversal, volatility, liquidity, turnover),
fundamental (accruals, profitability, asset growth, leverage, quality), and filing-derived.
4. Models: ridge/elastic-net baseline first, then gradient boosting (LightGBM); plus a deliberately
simple third benchmark so the note can honestly say how much the complex model adds.
5. Neutralization: regress the signal on market beta, size, and industry exposure; optimize the
portfolio to be market-, beta-, and sector-neutral directly.

06   P2: Alternative Data from SEC Filings (Weeks 11-16)
Alternative data is where free-market edges hide, because most quants with budget buy the
same commercial feeds. The SEC publishes everything, with timestamps, for free.
Directions (pick two, do not attempt all)
 Filing-tone / MD&A sentiment: language-model-based sentiment (FinBERT or a modern LLM)
on 10-K/10-Q MD&A sections, with a controlled, labeled evaluation set rather than eyeballed
samples.
 Risk-factor drift: measure how the set and language of risk factors changes between
consecutive 10-Ks. Rising specificity is a genuine, timestamped, underused signal.
 Earnings surprise from XBRL: surprise defined against a naive seasonal random walk rather
than a consensus estimate - free consensus data is the binding constraint.
 Filing-event study: 8-K event windows with abnormal-return measurement, doubling as a
validation harness for P1.
The rigor that matters here
    Point-in-time everything. Only use a document as of its actual filing/acceptance timestamp.
Indexing a document a model "should" have had earlier is the classic alt-data leak, and an
interviewer who does alt data will look for exactly this.
   Evaluation: a hand-labeled or rule-derived benchmark set, precision/recall/F1, and
comparison against baselines (naive, random, TF-IDF + logistic regression). A transformer that

07   P3: Transaction Cost, Market Impact & Capacity
(Weeks 17-20)
At a fund, a researcher whose job is to make sure the backtest survives contact with the
market is a real role. This is the single most common place amateur portfolios collapse under
questioning.
  Spread and fee model: commissions, exchange fees, half-spread crossing costs, market/limit
mix, borrow cost and locate for shorts - the short-side fee schedule is often overlooked and can
dominate.
 Market impact: implement the square-root law, impact ~ k * sigma * sqrt(Q / ADV), calibrate
k from your own volume data; compare against a linear-impact and constant-bps assumption.
 Turnover analysis: decomposition by rebalance frequency; show the net-alpha knee where
rebalancing stops paying.
 Capacity curve: performance as a function of capital, with ADV-based and participation-rate
caps. As capacity grows, impact grows, so alpha decays - plot the break-even AUM.
 De-risking simulation: shock the cost assumptions (2x, 3x slippage) and show which findings
survive. A strategy that only works at optimistic costs is not a strategy.
Deliverable: src/alpha/costs/ with the calibrated model, a figure set (equity curves gross vs
net, capacity curve, cost-sensitivity tornado), and a re-run of the P1 flagship with the
calibrated costs.
08   P4: Portfolio Construction & Risk (Weeks 21-24)
Turn a forecast into a portfolio someone could actually run, and prove you understand what
the portfolio's exposures actually are.
  Covariance estimation: sample covariance is unusable; use Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage and show the
eigenvalue instability of the sample matrix with a concrete blow-up illustration.
 Optimizers: mean-variance with shrinkage, minimum variance, risk parity, and a cost-aware
variant that penalizes turnover. Compare on net, not gross, performance.
 Risk measures: volatility, expected shortfall (CVaR), drawdown, and tracking error vs the
benchmark.
 Attribution: decompose realized P&L into signal, market/sector beta, style factors (size, value,
momentum, quality), and residual - the section where you show the "alpha" is not just a levered
momentum bet in disguise. Include the regression exposure table and contribution-by-factor
table.
 Stress testing: historical scenario replay (2008, 2020, 2022), a hypothetical scenario (rates
+200bp, spreads widen, correlation -> 1), and a sensitivity grid.
 Risk-adjusted selection: if the portfolio can only keep one model, choose on OOS net Sharpe
and capacity, not on the in-sample best.

The survivorship-bias trap - the most important data issue
 A free daily price source gives you the currently listed universe. Backtesting 15 years of "the
market" from that list means every delisted company - Enron, Lehman, MCI, Wirecard, and
thousands of small caps - is silently removed from history, which manufactures enormous
apparent returns. No quant reviewer will miss this if you present the equity curve.
Mitigations, in descending order of strength: (1) build the universe from a point-in-time
constituent file that includes delisted names (Ken French 3-factor universe, a delisting-return
database, or a hand-curated list of known delistings for your size tier - partial but honest); (2)
use a universe definition that is stable by construction, e.g. only names that have survived 3
years as of the date - a legitimate, point-in-time construct, documented as a survivorship-aware
approximation; (3) at minimum, explicitly disclose the bias, quantify it, and report results both
with and without the affected names. Write a paragraph titled "Survivorship bias" in the note.
Other traps: A 10-K for fiscal year 2023 filed in February 2024 cannot be used to make a
decision in January 2024 - use the filed date, always. S&P 500 and Russell membership files
are not freely available point-in-time - use an explicit universe rule instead of faking them.

11   The Validation Standard (Apply to Every Project)
    No feature uses information unavailable at decision time - verified by an automated as-of
assertion on a sample of rows.
   Universe membership is point-in-time, including delisted names, or the bias is disclosed and
quantified.
   Corporate actions handled; no raw-split price contamination.
   Purged K-fold with embargo; overlapping labels handled. Walk-forward OOS testing is the
headline result.
   All transformations (standardization, winsorization, neutralization, feature selection) fit on
train only.
   Multiple-comparison correction (deflated Sharpe or a documented FWER procedure)
reported alongside raw metrics.
   Random seeds fixed and recorded; results reproducible from a single command.
   Commissions, spread, impact, borrow, and financing costs modeled; turnover and capacity
reported.
   Regime and subperiod analysis, including the worst month; cost-sensitivity: does the result
survive 2x slippage?
   Benchmark table, exposure table (beta, sector, style loadings), and a failure analysis - what
did not work and what would falsify the result.

13   Presentation & Interview Prep
A hiring manager spends about ten minutes on the README and thirty on the research note.
Design for that. README, top to bottom:
 1. One paragraph: who you are, what you build, and the headline result in a number.
2. The results table (OOS IC, net Sharpe, turnover, capacity) with a link to the note.
3. The research note as a PDF or rendered page, and the reproduction command.
4. The project list with one line each.
5. "How to evaluate this work" - the specific questions a reviewer should probe, and where to
look for each. Inviting scrutiny is a strong move.
Do not include
 Jupyter notebooks with 40 cells and no narrative.
 Backtests that omit costs; model comparison tables without OOS splits.
 A README that opens with "This project explores the application of machine learning to stock
price prediction." A hiring quant reading that learns you were looking for a tutorial, not a
question.
Interview prep runs alongside from Week 3
Quant interviews test four things: probability and statistics, stochastic processes, mental math
under time pressure, and programming. Allocate roughly 20% of time to the technical track.
Keep a running log of every question you get wrong and re-solve it a week later - this
compounds faster than any book. Practice implementing formulas correctly under time
pressure; market-making and pair-trading questions are where candidates fail.

16   The Metrics That Matter
Metric
Why a Quant Cares
Out-of-sample rank IC and IC IR
Direct measure of predictive ordering, per-period and stable
Net-of-cost Sharpe / IR
The only Sharpe that means anything; gross is a fiction
Turnover and cost drag

Whether the edge is tradeable at any relevant size
